# Literature note: Neumark, Burn and Button (2019)

**Citation.** Neumark, D., Burn, I., Button, P. (2019). "Is It Harder for Older Workers to Find Jobs? New and Improved Evidence from a Field Experiment." Journal of Political Economy 127(2): 922-970.

**Version read.** NBER Working Paper 21669, October 2015, revised November 2017 (not the published JPE version; numbers may differ slightly from the journal).
**URL.** https://www.nber.org/papers/w21669 (PDF: https://www.nber.org/papers/w21669.pdf)
**Access date.** 2026-10-29
**What was read.** Full text of the working-paper PDF (text extracted with pdftotext). Page numbers below are the working paper's own page numbers; table numbers are the working paper's.

## Verified facts
- Design: a resume correspondence (audit) study of hiring; over 40,000 applications (40,223 resumes sent; Table 3). Abstract.
- Applicant ages are signalled through high-school graduation year. Age groups: young 29-31, middle 49-51, old 64-66 (Basic Parameters section; page not recorded).
- Occupations: administrative assistant (female applicants only), retail sales (male and female), security guard (male), janitor (male) (Data section text; Table 4 panels).
- Cities (Table 2): Sarasota, Miami, Pittsburgh, New York, Boston, Chicago, Charlotte, Phoenix, Birmingham, Houston, Salt Lake City, Los Angeles. These are US cities.
- Headline callback rates (Table 4, p. 25-26): all occupations pooled (Panel A) 18.69% young, 15.40% middle, 12.16% old; text describes this as about 18% and 35% lower for middle and old.
- Administrative, women (Panel B): 14.41% young, 10.30% middle, 7.58% old.
- Sales, men (Panel C): 20.89% young, 21.09% middle, 14.70% old.
- Sales, women (Panel D): 28.68% young, 25.87% middle, 18.43% old.
- Security, men (Panel E): 24.28%, 21.55%, 21.74%; differences only marginal or insignificant (p = 0.09 to 0.93).
- Janitors, men (Panel F): 32.08%, 33.45%, 25.89%; old significantly lower.
- Gender (text, p. 26-27; Table 5): the authors state age discrimination is stronger for women than for men in sales, and stronger in the female-dominated job. Probit marginal effects for old vs. young: women in sales -0.095, men in sales -0.044-0.047 (Table 5). Abstract: robust evidence against older women, "considerably less" against men after bias correction.
- Callback rates are higher for women than men at all ages (footnote 43, p. 26).

## Is the team's sentence accurate?
Draft: "Neumark et al. (2019) document hiring discrimination that differs by gender and age."
Mostly accurate but vague and slightly incomplete: it omits that the study is an experiment (callbacks, not hires) and the direction of the differences. Gender comparisons are partly confounded with occupation (administrative is women only; security and janitor men only; only sales has both sexes).

**Corrected sentence:** "In a US resume correspondence experiment with over 40,000 applications to four low-skill occupations, Neumark et al. (2019) find that callback rates fall with applicant age (29-31 vs. 49-51 vs. 64-66), with stronger and more robust evidence of age discrimination against women than against men."

## Caveats / what the paper does not show
- Only US cities and four occupations (administrative, sales, security, janitor); no evidence for other occupations, high-skill jobs, or other countries.
- Outcome is employer callbacks, not job offers or hires; the authors discuss this limit.
- No analysis of vacancy text or posted age/gender preferences. China appears only in the literature review (a mention of job boards in China and Mexico where employers state age preferences, in the reference-list/discussion text; page not recorded); this paper provides no Chinese evidence.
- Gender differences are within-occupation only in sales; elsewhere sex and occupation are confounded by design.
- Working-paper version read; check the JPE version before citing exact figures.
