# Lit note: Burn et al. (2022) and the companion applicant-behaviour paper

Verified 2026-10-29. Page numbers below are PDF page numbers of the NBER files, not printed journal pages.

## Paper A (the paper the draft cites)

- Citation (as given by the team): Burn, I., Button, P., Munguia Corella, L. F., Neumark, D. (2022). "Older Workers Need Not Apply? Ageist Language in Job Ads and Age Discrimination in Hiring." Journal of Labor Economics 40(3).
- Title check: the title ends in a question mark and does not read "Does Ageist Language in Job Ads Predict Age Discrimination in Hiring?". Use the title above.
- Journal details (volume, issue, pages, DOI): NOT verified. The publisher page returned HTTP 403 and search did not confirm them. Check on the journal site before citing.
- Version read: NBER Working Paper 26552, December 2019, revised May 2020 (not the published JLE version). Text differences from the journal version are possible.
- URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w26552 ; PDF https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26552/w26552.pdf
- Access date: 2026-10-29
- What was read: full text (55-page PDF, extracted with pdftotext). Not read: the published JLE article.

### Verified facts
- The paper links ad text to employer callbacks from a resume correspondence experiment (Neumark, Burn, Button 2019). It asks which age stereotypes in ads predict lower callbacks for older fictitious applicants (abstract, p.1-2 of the paper).
- Abstract finding: stereotype language "sometimes predicts" discrimination against older workers. For men, health, personality and skill stereotypes all predict it. For women, only personality stereotypes. Evidence is much stronger for men.
- Main estimates are in Table 4 (PDF p.48, by gender, age group and occupation; clustered SEs, only some coefficients significant at 5%) and Table 5 (PDF p.49, discrimination against younger applicants).
- The unit of analysis is the employer's callback to fictitious applicants. The paper has no data on real applicants. It says that without applicant data the analysis is limited (around line 419 of the extracted text, PDF p.9-10).
- Applicant behaviour appears only as a hypothesis. The paper notes that employers might use ageist wording to discourage older applicants (PDF p.8-9), and its conclusion mentions this as a policy concern (near the end of the main text). It does not test it.

### Corrected one-sentence description
Burn et al. (2022) link the wording of job ads to employer callbacks in a resume field experiment and find that some age-stereotyped language in ads predicts lower callbacks for older applicants, more clearly for men.

## Paper B (fits the draft sentence about application behaviour)

- Citation: Burn, I., Firoozi, D., Ladd, D., Neumark, D. (2022). "Help Really Wanted? The Impact of Age Stereotypes in Job Ads on Applications from Older Workers." NBER Working Paper 30287, July 2022. (Journal publication status: not checked.)
- URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w30287 ; PDF https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30287/w30287.pdf
- Access date: 2026-10-29
- What was read: full text (PDF, pdftotext).

### Verified facts
- Design: the researchers wrote artificial ads for administrative assistant, retail and security guard jobs. They randomly varied ageist stereotype language and studied the ages of real applicants (abstract).
- Table 3, top panel (PDF p.47): with any ageist-language treatment, average applicant age is 2.7 years lower (coefficient -2.687) and the share over 40 is 9.4 percentage points lower (-0.094). The estimates cover 228 ad-level observations (city-occupation-ad cells).
- Table 3, bottom panel: the machine-learning phrases alone give about -2.2 years and -7.9 points. The AARP-phrase arm adds a further -2.3 years and -7.7 points.
- Table 4 (PDF p.47): single-stereotype arms (communication, physical, technology) are weaker statistically. Share over 40 is lower by 4.4 to 8.2 points, and none is significant at 5% in two-sided tests.
- Text, PDF p.22: ads with all three machine-learning phrases give about 2.5 years younger applicants and 11.7 points fewer over-40 applicants.
- Table 3, last column: no evidence that applicants withheld their age in response to the ads.
- Text, PDF p.27-28: the authors compare the reduction in applicants over 40 (4.41 points, from a control share of 20.00%) with the callback shortfall for older applicants in the correspondence study (4.91 points). They conclude the two effects are of similar size, in their experimental setting.

### Corrected one-sentence description
Burn, Firoozi, Ladd and Neumark (2022) show in a randomised field experiment with artificial ads that ageist wording lowers the average age of real applicants and the share aged over 40.

## Is the team's draft sentence accurate?
"Burn et al. (2022) show that age-stereotyped advertisements affect application behavior." Not accurate for Burn, Button, Munguia Corella, Neumark (2022), which is about employer callbacks. It is accurate for Burn, Firoozi, Ladd, Neumark (2022). Suggested fix: cite Burn, Firoozi, Ladd and Neumark (2022, NBER WP 30287) for application behaviour, and Burn, Button, Munguia Corella and Neumark (2022, JLE) for the ad language and callback link.

## Caveats
- Paper A: working-paper version read, not the published JLE text. JLE volume, pages and DOI unverified.
- Paper B: only working-paper status verified. The experiment covers three occupations and a limited set of cities. It measures the age of applicants who responded to the ads. It does not measure hiring outcomes.
- The 11.7-point figure applies only to the three-phrase arm. The pooled any-treatment estimate is 9.4 points.
- Page references are PDF page numbers. Table 3 and Table 4 in Paper B were located by pdftotext page breaks and may be off by one.
