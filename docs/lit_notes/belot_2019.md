# Lit note: Belot, Kircher and Muller (2019)

**Citation:** Belot, M., Kircher, P. and Muller, P. (2019). Providing Advice to Jobseekers at Low Cost: An Experimental Study on Online Advice. Review of Economic Studies 86(4), 1411-1447.

**Version read:** IZA Discussion Paper No. 10068, July 2016 (earlier working-paper version, not the journal text).
**URL:** https://docs.iza.org/dp10068.pdf
**Access date:** 2026-10-29
**What was read:** the full PDF text (extracted with pdftotext): abstract, introduction, sample and design, results (Tables 4, 9-12, 22). The Edinburgh homepage 2018 version could not be fetched (connection refused), so numbers below are from the 2016 version and may differ in the published paper. Page numbers are the printed numbers in the paper's footer; Tables 10-12 sit on pp. 30-31.

## Verified facts
- Intervention: a web job-search tool. Participants name an occupation and the tool lists related occupations (from observed occupational transitions and skill similarity) and returns vacancies across them (Intro, p. 2). Control used a standard keyword/occupation search. Advice only, no sanctions or monitoring.
- Design: randomized; 12 weekly lab sessions at Edinburgh; everyone used the standard interface in weeks 1-3, then half were offered the alternative interface from week 4 (Sec. 5, p. 3 of intro and Sec. 5).
- Sample: about 300 lab participants (p. 2), unemployed, recruited mostly (86%) at Edinburgh Job Centres; 18+, no age cap (Sec. 5.3). Mean age 36, 43% female (Table 4, p. ~20). Self-selected, not representative (authors say so).
- Outcomes: vacancies listed, applications, interviews, from lab logs and weekly surveys (including search outside the lab).
- Occupational broadness of listed jobs rose by 0.2 SD (p. 3).
- Interviews: total interviews +44% (Table 10 col. 3, p. 30; significant only at 10%, SE 0.28). Applications: total effect -0.02, not significant (Table 22).
- Subgroups: occupationally narrow searchers +75% interviews (Table 10, p<0.05); broad searchers +12%, not significant. Long-unemployed (above median, over 80 days) narrow searchers largest: +156% total, +106% survey/outside-lab (Table 11, p. 31, p<0.01). Short-unemployed narrow searchers +34%, not significant.
- No significant effect on wages of listed, applied or interviewed jobs (p. 32). Job offers and job finding were not shown to change; authors say a longer follow-up and larger sample are needed.

## Is the draft sentence accurate?
Broadly yes: "tailored job-search information can improve interviews for some jobseekers" matches the abstract and Tables 10-11. It is vague, and should say interviews rather than outcomes, and name the subgroup. It should not imply job finding.

**Corrected one-sentence description:** In a randomized lab experiment with about 300 unemployed jobseekers in Edinburgh, Belot et al. (2019) found that an online tool suggesting related occupations broadened search and raised reported job interviews (about 44% overall, concentrated among narrow searchers unemployed for some months), without showing effects on job offers or employment.

## Caveats and what the paper does not show
- Not about age: age is only a control variable (Table 22, coefficients insignificant); no age-group heterogeneity was reported in what I read. Sample has mean age 36.
- Not China, not any non-UK setting.
- Not about job-ad wording; the intervention is occupation suggestions, not ad text.
- Not a chatbot or LLM; it is a rule-based tool using labour-market statistics.
- Small sample, overall interview effect only marginally significant (10%); subgroup effects come from post-hoc splits with low baseline interview counts (lab baseline 0.09 per week; footnote 42 notes the huge lab effect reflects a tiny base).
- Verified from the 2016 IZA version only; check the published RES version before citing exact figures.
